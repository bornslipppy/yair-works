Every enterprise now runs on a dozen AI tools — coding agents, copilots, internal agents — each with its own keys, bills and blind spots. Wonderful AI Gateway is the single layer every one of those requests passes through, on its way to Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and the rest.

I designed the product from the first brief to a working, tested prototype: the place where an organisation decides who may use which model, how much, for what — and what happens when a request crosses the line.

The problem

AI usage was outpacing AI control. Teams adopted tools independently, so nobody could answer basic questions: which team spent the most last month, what employees were pasting into prompts, whether we could prove who accessed what if an auditor asked.

The obvious answer — a spend dashboard — would only have described the damage after it happened. A gateway sits in the path of every request. That makes it an enforcement point, not a report, and it changed what the product had to be.

The reframe

I organised the product around one object: the request. Every surface answers a question about it — who sent it (Usage), whether it was allowed (Model access), whether it was safe (Governance), whether it was affordable (Spend limits), and where it should go (Model router).

Policy is authored once and enforced before the request leaves the perimeter. Analytics exist to explain enforcement, not to replace it.

Decisive choices

  1. Guardrails that fail closed — with a graded response

    A safety control that only knows "block" gets switched off the first time it blocks the CFO. Each policy declares what it detects (a pattern or a described topic) and what happens on a match: Block, Warn, or reroute. Credentials are blocked outright; personal and health identifiers warn and redact. Wonderful-maintained defaults ship on, and admins add their own beside them rather than editing ours.

  2. A cap is a ceiling, not an allocation

    The most consequential misreading in the product: an admin sets a $3,000 cap for premium models under a $5,000 limit and believes the other $2,000 is now "reserved" for everything else. It isn't. I designed the rule editor, its summary and its validation so the consequence of a rule is legible before save — who it governs, what it limits, and exactly what happens at the limit.

  3. Degrade, don't break

    Hitting a limit or a provider outage shouldn't stop someone's work mid-task. Rules can move eligible requests to a lower-cost model instead of blocking, and failover chains reroute automatically when a provider goes down. Safety and continuity stop being a trade-off.

  4. Never flag spend in isolation

    Two engineers can each spend $27K in a month — one shipped an internal CRM, the other shipped nothing. An alert that treats them the same teaches people to ignore alerts. I killed an early "Insights" surface that did exactly that, and moved toward pairing burn with return: what the inference was for.

  5. Reversible by default

    Access changes ripple across hundreds of people. Bulk edits list every affected model and audience before confirming, and every save offers Undo. Admins act faster when mistakes are cheap.

How I worked

No static mockups. I built the product as a working front-end prototype across eight surfaces, so engineering, sales and customers reacted to real behaviour — filters that compose, rules that validate, limits that expire.

I wrote briefs as outcomes and constraints rather than screens, then held the prototype to them with an automated suite: 57 tests, including 5,110 accounting reconciliations that check every filtered total still adds up. A navigation review reproduced each issue with exact steps before anything was fixed.

What I didn't build

No shared group wallets — limits govern each person, so one heavy user can't drain a team. No reserved budget slices, and no artificial "remainder" maths. No throttling, which hid limits instead of explaining them. Each was requested; each would have made the policy harder to predict. A safeguard people can't predict is a safeguard they route around.
